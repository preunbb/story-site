## Chapter 1 — Saturday Morning

Bonnie Mercer had been making her boys breakfast for twenty-one years, and on the morning of the fourth of July she was still bad at it.

Not the *food*. The food was fine. She made fluffy pancakes that picked up the syrup like a sponge, and her bacon was good, even, three-strip slabs that she fanned out on a folded paper towel because Patrick — God rest him, even after the divorce — had always said grease made him queasy. The pancakes were fine. The boys would eat the pancakes.

The problem was that Bonnie made the pancakes in a kitchen built for one slim woman, while she herself was no longer slim and had honestly never been small. She was five-foot-four and somewhere on the wrong side of one-seventy, and the soft pillow that had once been her waist had thickened into a shelf that her boobs sat on when she leaned over the stove, which made her have to lean *farther* over the stove, which made her hip stick out further behind her, which was how a stack of clean Tupperware on the island ended up on the kitchen floor at six forty-five in the morning while she swore quietly into a bowl of batter.

“Shoot. *Shoot.*”

“Mom.” Caleb shuffled in, scratching his stomach, one eye still glued shut. He was twenty-one and he stood in the doorway like a bear that had wandered too close to a campsite — broad shoulders, low-slung gray sweatpants, no shirt, no shame. “Mom, it’s, like, six.”

“Six *forty-five,* honey. Go sit down. I’m making you the good kind.”

“I don’t need the good kind. I need to *sleep.*”

“You can sleep when you’re dead, sweetie. Sit.”

He yawned so hard his jaw cracked, and obediently dropped into a chair at the breakfast nook. The chair scraped. Everything in this kitchen scraped or slid or rattled. The house had been built in 1962 and the linoleum had been laid by a man who clearly hated linoleum.

Jonah appeared behind his brother a minute later, blinking through his bangs. Nineteen, narrow through the chest, soft around the jaw still, with the kind of polite tired face that always made Bonnie want to feed him soup. He was wearing flannel pajama pants and one of his dad’s old Garmin Tech t-shirts, which was three sizes too big and hung off him like a tarp.

“Morning, Mom.”

“Morning, sweetheart. Sit. Coffee’s in the pot.”

“I love you,” Jonah said, in the heartfelt voice he reserved for coffee.

Bonnie smiled into the batter, and the warmth of it nearly took her out at the knees. *Both* her boys, home, sitting at her table for the first weekend since Christmas. Caleb had finished his junior year at State, Jonah had finished freshman year at the same place a week behind him, and now they were both here, in *her* kitchen, smelling like sleep and her good detergent, complaining about the hour. She would have made them ten breakfasts. She would have made them *fifty*.

“Are you crying?” Caleb said.

“I’m *cooking,*” Bonnie said.

“She’s crying,” Jonah confirmed, settling down across from him with two mugs.

“I am *cooking,* Caleb Andrew Mercer. Eat your fruit.”

She turned, fast — too fast — to grab the cast iron off the back burner, and her hip caught the silverware tray. Forks rang on the floor like wind chimes. Both boys jumped.

“I’ve got it,” Jonah said, already standing.

“I’ve *got* it, baby.”

“Mom—”

“Sit *down.*”

She crouched, picked up the forks, dropped one again, picked it up again, set them all in the sink, washed her hands, and turned back to the stove with her cheeks pink and her bun half-falling out. Her glasses had slid down her nose and she didn’t have a free hand to push them up. She blinked the kitchen back into focus over the rim.

It was going to be a good day. She had *decided* it was going to be a good day.

She lifted the cast iron skillet off the burner. It was the heavy one — the heirloom one from her own mother, twelve inches across, four pounds without bacon in it, almost six with. The handle was a good foot long and stuck out off the back of the pan like a tiller. She’d been swinging it around this kitchen since she was twenty-three years old and she still, somehow, kept forgetting how *far* the handle stuck out.

“Caleb, baby, scoot down a chair, I’m bringing you the plate.”

“I’m fine where I am.”

“You’re right in the way, sweetie.”

“I’m *fine.*”

He was not fine. He was sitting in the chair closest to the stove, the one with its back almost against the counter, with his big sleepy legs splayed wide. The waistband of his sweatpants had drifted down past his hipbones and the dark soft shape of him was very obviously slung loose down the inside of his left thigh under thin gray cotton. Bonnie averted her eyes the way mothers of grown sons learn to.

“Move, baby. The pan’s hot.”

“I’m not in the *way*, Mom, just bring it.”

“*Caleb.*”

“*Mom.*”

She huffed, hefted the skillet up off the burner with her right hand around the long handle, and pivoted, ninety degrees, to walk it across to the breakfast nook.

She forgot about the handle.

The handle of a twelve-inch cast iron skillet, swung in a one-eighty pivot at hip height by a five-foot-four woman with a generous bust, sweeps through approximately the same arc as a small child on a swing. Bonnie did her pivot. The pan came around. The pan reached the stove’s edge. Bonnie kept turning, because she was looking at her destination — the breakfast nook — and not at her pan.

The blunt iron tip of the handle reached, and continued, and the next thing in its way was Caleb’s lap.

He’d half-stood, finally, to take the pan from her. He was leaning forward across the corner of the table. His sweatpants were sagged down to mid-hip. His balls were hanging unprotected in the cotton at the very front of his crotch, low and loose with sleep, the right one a little forward of the left like always — *family trait,* Patrick used to joke, *the Mercer lopside.*

The iron handle caught him exactly there.

*Squick.*

Not a heavy sound. A *wet* sound. The handle was blunt and rounded and warm from sitting on a burner, and it hit not the side of his sack but the front, full-on, square. Bonnie felt the resistance through the cast iron — a soft *give* and then a sudden *not-give*, like a grape under a thumb that refuses and refuses and then doesn’t — and she stopped, mid-pivot, the pan still moving forward an inch into him before her hand finally caught up to what her body had just done.

Caleb didn’t scream.

He made a sound she had never heard a person make. A small high *huh,* almost a question, the same noise he’d made at four years old when he’d fallen off the playscape and looked up at her with no air in his lungs. Then his eyes rolled up into his head and he sat back down. Hard. Onto the chair, then half-off the chair. His knees clamped together a beat too late, like he’d only just remembered they could.

The skillet tipped. Bacon grease sluiced out the front lip of it and onto the front of his sweatpants, dark grease soaking through gray cotton straight down into the lap that the iron had just punched into.

“Oh god,” Bonnie said.

“*Mom—*” Jonah was already up.

“Oh *god* — oh *honey* — oh god, oh god, *oh god* —”

Caleb was making the *huh, huh, huh* noise on a loop now, faster, like a baby bird, and his hands were finally moving, one to his lap and one over his mouth, and Bonnie set the skillet down on the table — she didn’t even register it scorching the wood, she would notice the burn ring three days later and start crying again — and lurched around the corner of the breakfast nook with her hand outstretched toward her son.

“Don’t — don’t —” Jonah was saying, but she couldn’t hear him, she had to *see,* she had to *help.* She crouched in front of Caleb’s chair and her hand went to the front of his sweatpants because all she could think was the *grease,* the grease was burning him, she had to —

“*Don’t touch it,*” Caleb wheezed.

She froze.

“Don’t — don’t — *Mom* — don’t touch it.”

His voice was a string pulled all the way taut and his eyes were enormous and shiny and he was breathing through his nose in tight little fast breaths and his big shoulders were *shaking.* The grease was already cooling into his sweatpants. Through the wet cotton she could see, very clearly, the shape of his sack pulled up tight against his body — except for the right side. The right side was hanging *low.* And it was the wrong shape. It wasn’t a round shape anymore. It was — softer than that. Wider. Like a slow water balloon.

“Oh, *honey,*” Bonnie whispered.

“It’s okay,” Caleb said, with no air. “It’s okay. Mom. *Mom.* It’s okay. It was an accident.”

“It was an accident,” she repeated, and burst into tears.

Jonah, behind her, said quietly, in the patient careful voice he used to use when he had to walk her through the cable box, “Mom. Where’s your phone.”

“On — on the charger — in the —”

“I’ll get it.”

He stepped past her. Caleb opened one eye and looked at his mom. The eye was wet. The eye was full of all kinds of things — pain that hadn’t fully arrived yet, the slow cold understanding of what he could feel in his lap, and underneath all of it the same exhausted patient love he had given her for twenty-one years.

“Mom,” he said. “I’m okay. Quit *crying.*”

Bonnie, kneeling on her own kitchen floor at six fifty-three on a Saturday morning, sobbed her eyes out into her son’s greasy lap, and tried to believe him.

## Chapter 2 — The Stairs

They got him to the couch.

It took a while. Caleb couldn’t straighten up — every time he tried, the breath went out of him and the *huh huh huh* started again — and Bonnie was no help because Bonnie kept apologizing in long unbroken strings, and Jonah, all of nineteen, was the only one in the room who seemed to remember how to do anything. He got an arm under his brother’s armpit. He told Bonnie, very gently and very firmly, to “sit down on a *different* chair, Mom, just — sit down for a sec, please,” and she did, on the chair by the back door, with her hands pressed over her mouth.

He walked Caleb to the couch one shuffling foot at a time. He propped him with two pillows under his knees. He went to the freezer and came back with a bag of peas and a clean dishtowel.

“I’m okay,” Caleb said, eyes shut, head all the way back.

“You’re not okay,” Jonah said.

“I’m okay,” Caleb said, and started crying.

Bonnie heard him cry and put her face in her hands at the kitchen table. The bacon was still uncooked in the skillet she had set down on the wood. The grease had spread out in a long brown stain. Outside it was already eighty degrees and the morning sun was coming in yellow through the screen door and somewhere down the block a neighbor was setting off bottle rockets early, the small dry *pop* of them, and every time one went off Bonnie flinched.

“Mom.” Jonah was at her elbow. She hadn’t heard him cross the kitchen.

“I’m so sorry, baby.”

“Mom, I think — I think we should drive him in.”

“To — to *urgent care?* Honey, it’s — he’ll be okay. He just needs ice. I’ll get him ice. He just—”

“Mom.” Jonah crouched next to her, two big patient brown eyes the same color as her own. He was the only one in the house who could ever calm her down. He had been a quiet child. He’d learned early. “Mom. It’s — it isn’t the right shape.”

Bonnie covered her mouth.

“I think it ruptured, Mom.”

“Oh, *god.*”

“It’s okay. They’re — they fix that. He’ll be okay. I just think we should go.”

She nodded, because Jonah was telling her to nod, and Jonah was nineteen years old and was the only adult in this kitchen anymore. He squeezed her hand.

“Can you go get him pants?” he said. “Some — some loose ones. And the — there’s those old, like, athletic supporter things in Dad’s drawer in the basement. The compression kind. He’ll be more comfortable, riding in.”

“The — the jockstrap?”

“Yeah. The big kind. The one with the cup. Just — bring it down, okay? I’ll get the car started.”

“Okay, baby. Okay.”

She stood up, wobbly. She kissed Jonah on the top of the head — he was kneeling, she could reach — and then she padded out of the kitchen into the dining room and across the runner and up the stairs.

The carpet runner on the stairs was thirty years old. Bonnie and Patrick had laid it themselves the year they moved in, with a tack hammer and a borrowed staple gun and a half a case of cheap red wine. It had pulled loose on the bottom landing maybe four years ago. It had been a little crooked ever since. Bonnie kept meaning to call the carpet guy. She kept *not* calling the carpet guy. She passed it every day.

She didn’t catch on it on the way up.

She came down with the jockstrap in one hand and a pair of Caleb’s old basketball shorts in the other and a clean undershirt under her arm and her glasses sliding down her nose again, and she was *crying* — quietly, just leaking, the way she leaked at sad commercials — and she did not see the rucked-up edge of the runner on the bottom landing.

Caleb had heard her on the stairs. He’d hauled himself up off the couch, one hand braced on the back of it and one hand cupping the heavy *wrong* weight in the front of his pants, and shuffled over to meet her, because his mom should not have to carry his things to him, his mom had cried enough today already. He was standing at the foot of the stairs with one hand out for the bundle of clothes.

“Mom, you don’t have to — *Mom.*”

Bonnie’s slipper caught on the runner.

There was a moment, a long unfair moment, when she felt it catch and felt her weight come forward and felt her hands rise instinctively to brace and felt the clothes start to leave her fingers and realized she was going *down* and there was nothing in the world she could do about it, no railing on the inside of the landing because they’d taken the railing out in 2009 to fit Patrick’s recliner through, no son fast enough to catch her, no air left in her lungs to even shout. She came forward off the bottom step at a falling-tree angle, all her soft top-heavy weight tipping out over the empty air in front of her, and the only thing in the room directly in front of her at the height of her descending knee was her oldest son.

“*Caleb—*”

Her right knee hit him in the lap from above.

The angle of it was the cruelest possible angle. Caleb had his weight on his back foot already, half-turned to take the clothes from her, his front leg slightly out for balance and his sweatpants — the new ones, the loose ones, the ones Jonah hadn’t put on him yet — riding even lower at the front than they had been at the breakfast table. His sack was *out.* It was *visible,* in the open vee at the front of the waistband, the dark heavy weight of him hanging loose and forward, the right side already ruptured and softening, the left side still proudly intact — still a *ball*, still round, still the shape of a testicle — and that was the side Bonnie’s knee came down on.

She landed.

All of her. Her whole hundred and seventy-something pounds of soft frantic mom, hands full of jockstrap, no brace, no slowing-down, her right knee leading like a pile-driver and her hip and chest right behind it.

The knee landed on his left nut.

The left nut had no warning at all. It was a healthy twenty-one-year-old testicle, an inch and change across, sitting loose and low in the cool of the morning, the *good* one, the unhurt one, the one Caleb had been quietly putting all of his hope into for the last forty-five minutes — *at least I’ve still got the lefty, at least I’ll still have one.* It made it through one quarter of one second of compression. Her kneecap drove it back into the front of his pelvis and *kept going,* because her whole weight was still falling. There was a moment where Caleb felt it flatten — felt it *flatten,* the awful unmistakable feeling of a part of his own body taking a shape it should not take — and then it didn’t flatten any further because it couldn’t. There was no give left. His pelvis was bone and her knee was bone and his testicle was in between them and his testicle was the part of the equation that was not bone.

*Crunch.*

The sound was *not* a wet sound. It was a dry, gritty, *granular* sound, like a walnut between two flat stones, and it was a sound Bonnie felt with her *kneecap* before she heard it with her ear.

Caleb went down.

He went down so completely and so silently that for a second Bonnie thought she had killed him. He didn’t scream. He didn’t *huh huh* this time. He went *backward,* arms out, eyes white-open and unseeing, and his back hit the floor flat and his head bounced once on the rug and his knees folded inward over his ruined groin in a slow doll-like collapse, and he made one tiny sound, a single thin *oh,* almost peaceful, the kind of sound a man makes when something has finally finished happening to him.

Bonnie landed on top of him.

Her bust hit his sternum. Her clothes-and-jockstrap bundle went one way, her glasses went the other. Her knee, the one that had just *crunched* her oldest son’s last surviving testicle, was still planted in his crotch, and she felt the soft *give* of him under her kneecap and she scrabbled to get her weight off but the slipper that had betrayed her was still hooked on the runner and her other foot couldn’t find the floor and she could not, for two long awful breathing seconds, *get her knee off her son.*

“*Mom!*” Jonah was running in from the kitchen. “Mom, *Mom—*”

She heaved sideways. The slipper finally tore free. She rolled off Caleb’s chest onto the hardwood and her elbow slammed into the leg of the side table and the lamp wobbled and she screamed, not at the elbow, at her son, at her *son*, who was lying on her hallway floor with his sweatpants soaked grease-brown and his eyes pointed at the ceiling and a small thin string of drool running from the corner of his mouth into his ear.

“*Caleb. Caleb, baby, Caleb — Caleb, oh god, oh god, oh god—*”

“Mom.” Jonah was on the floor now too, on the other side of his brother, hands hovering, not touching. “Mom, *Mom,* I need you to — I need you to get up. Mom. Get up, please. I’m calling 911.”

“No, no, no — we were going to *drive* him —”

“Mom. *Get up.* I’m calling 911.”

He was already dialing. His hands were steady. She didn’t know how his hands were steady. She knelt on her own hallway floor with one slipper off and one slipper on and her glasses crooked across her face and a long string of snot connecting her chin to the back of Caleb’s shirt, and she looked down at her boy — her firstborn, her brown-haired baby, the one she’d nursed for fourteen straight months because he wouldn’t take a bottle — and she looked at the shape of him in his sweatpants and there was no shape *at all* anymore down there, no ball, no two balls, no plums, nothing — just a soft swollen *bag* hanging dark and wrong in the cotton, with a slow dark wet patch blooming through the front from where his right one had been seeping into the fabric for half an hour.

“I broke him,” she whispered. “Oh my god. Oh my god, oh my god, I broke him.”

“Hi, yeah, I need an ambulance —” Jonah said into his phone, in a level voice she had never heard him use, “—my brother’s been hit really hard in the groin, twice. I think his testicles are — I think they’re ruptured. Both of them. Yeah. Yeah, both of them. He’s conscious but he’s — he might be in shock. We’re at 412 Aspen. Yeah. Mercer. Yeah, please.”

Bonnie put her forehead against Caleb’s shoulder and sobbed.

Caleb, who had been quiet for almost a full minute now, who had been somewhere far away inside his own head, somewhere very dark and very cold and very quiet, came back enough to feel her crying on him. He lifted one hand. It cost him visibly to lift the one hand. He set it on her hair.

“Mom,” he said.

She wailed.

“Mom. *Stop.* It’s — it’s okay.”

“It’s *not* okay—”

“It’s an *accident,* Mom. It’s — it’s — *accidents happen.* It’s okay.”

“Both of them, baby. Both of them, oh my god, both of them —”

“It’s — yeah.” He swallowed. His throat clicked. “Yeah, I — I felt that one. Yeah.” And he turned his face toward the wall, very slowly, because he did not want his mother to see the look that had just come into his eyes, and he kept his hand on her hair and he kept it very gentle, and he breathed in, and he breathed out, and he did not say anything else for a long time.

## Chapter 3 — Urgent Care

The ambulance took Caleb.

Jonah and Bonnie followed it.

Jonah drove. Of course Jonah drove. Jonah had had his license for two years and four months and had nine hundred miles of confident careful highway under his belt; Bonnie had had her license for twenty-seven years and was, in this particular moment, not allowed to be trusted within twenty feet of a steering wheel. He helped her into the passenger side. He put her purse in her lap. He buckled her in himself, because her hands had stopped working. He pulled the minivan out of the driveway in a careful three-point turn and followed the ambulance’s red-and-white lights down Aspen and onto Main.

Bonnie cried for the first six minutes. Then she ran out of crying. She sat with the seatbelt cutting across her chest and her hands flat on her thighs and her glasses fogging up and unfogging in the AC, and she watched the back of the ambulance, and she did not blink.

“Mom,” Jonah said at the second red light. “Mom, listen. They’re going to fix him. They fix this all the time. He’s twenty-one. He’s — he’s healthy. He’s going to be okay.”

“Both of them, baby.”

“I know.”

“*Both* of them.”

“I *know,* Mom.”

“Honey, what if he can’t — what if he doesn’t — what about kids — what about—”

“Mom,” Jonah said, and his voice was tight for the first time. “Mom. *Stop.* Please.”

She stopped. She watched the ambulance. She put her hand on his on the gear shift and he didn’t take his away and they drove the last four blocks like that.

They got to the urgent care behind St. Margaret’s — the ambulance had been routed there because the ER at County was on diversion — and they parked in the family lot and Jonah came around to her side and got her out of the van the way he used to get his grandma out of the van, with a hand under her elbow and a careful tilt of his shoulder for her to lean on. She was crying again by the time they hit the automatic doors.

Inside it was very bright and very cold and the waiting room was almost empty — a fourth of July morning, no one had managed to ruin themselves yet. A heavyset older woman behind the desk looked up over her bifocals and her face did something complicated.

“Mercer?” she said.

“Yes,” Jonah said. “We — they brought my brother in just now—”

“They’ve got him in two. Doctor’s with him. You can — go ahead and sit, honey, I’ve got some forms for you, I’ll come around in a minute.”

“Thank you.”

He led Bonnie to the chairs along the wall — the cheap blue plastic kind, bolted in rows of four — and sat her down in the seat nearest the wall. He sat next to her. He put his arm around her.

She wept.

She wept silently, with her glasses off, with her face in her hands, with her shoulders going. Jonah held her. She had been holding *him* since 2006, and now her nineteen-year-old held her in a row of urgent-care chairs while her oldest son was in a room down the hall with a doctor’s gloved hand on him learning whether or not he would ever be a father.

After a few minutes Bonnie sat up. She pulled herself together the way she always pulled herself together after a cry — she sat up straight, she patted her cheeks dry with the heel of her hand, she pushed her hair off her face, she put her glasses back on. She blew her nose into a Kleenex from her purse. She balled the Kleenex up in her fist.

“I need another one,” she said quietly.

“Here.” Jonah held out the small travel pack.

“No, baby, I’ve got my big ones in the —” She turned to dig in her purse. Her purse was the big tan slouchy one Patrick had bought her for Mother’s Day six years ago, the one with the chunky brass-finish hardware on the front flap. She always kept it on the floor between her feet or hung from her shoulder by the long strap. It was sitting on the floor now between her ankles. She reached for it, and when she sat back up she lifted it by the strap and turned, simultaneously, toward Jonah, who was still sitting next to her with his legs apart and his hands folded between his knees, his head down, exhausted.

The full weight of the purse — three pounds, easy, with the wallet and the makeup bag and the paperback novel and the half-pack of trail mix and the keys — swung on the long strap.

The brass-finish hardware on the front flap is what got him.

It was a chunky decorative metal piece, an inch and a half wide, a flat squared-off latch with a raised edge. It came around on its arc and into Jonah’s lap at exactly the height of his lap, and it hit the inside left thigh of his flannel pajama pants first and then, with a tiny shift of momentum, it continued upward and inward and *thunked* into the soft loose pouch of his pajama pants at the front of his crotch.

The metal hit his right testicle.

It hit it square. It hit it dead-on. The latch wasn’t sharp — it was blunt and squared off — but it was *heavy* and it was *swinging* and it had three pounds of momentum behind it, and Jonah’s right ball was a healthy unsuspecting nineteen-year-old testicle in nothing but a layer of flannel and a thin layer of boxer-brief jersey, and the latch caught it at the very front of his sack and crushed it into his groin and the brass corner — there was a corner, there was a *little raised edge* — bit *in*.

*Pop.*

Not a big *pop.* A small one. A muffled wet polite little *pop*, the same noise a single grape makes underfoot when you don’t mean to step on it.

Jonah went *“—!”*

Just that. Just the inhale.

Bonnie froze with her purse halfway around its arc, and her face did something it had not done all morning. It didn’t crumble. It didn’t do *anything.* It just emptied out. Every muscle in it sat very still. Her eyes were very wide and the rims were red and she was holding her breath. She watched her younger son.

Jonah did not move for one second. Two seconds. Three. His hands were still folded between his knees. His head was still bent. And then, very slowly, like a building settling, he bent forward at the waist, all the way down, until his forehead was on his knees, and his hands came up — not to his lap, he didn’t go for his lap, he didn’t want to *touch* it — and pressed flat over his eyes.

“Mom,” he said into his knees, in a voice that was very small and very calm and very very far away. “Mom. Don’t.”

“Oh my god,” Bonnie whispered.

“Mom. *Don’t.*”

“Oh my god — *baby* — oh my god — Jonah — *Jonah, baby—*”

She let go of the purse. The purse hit the carpet with a soft *whump.* She put both her hands on the back of her son’s neck. Her son who had been holding *her,* one minute earlier, and was now folded in half over his own lap with the right side of his pajama pants going slowly and visibly dark in a small wet bloom around the front seam.

The woman behind the front desk stood up.

“Honey?” she called. “Honey, what’s — what just happened?”

“My — *my purse* —” Bonnie said, and the word *purse* came out as a sob. “I — I — *my purse—*”

“Oh, *honey.*” The woman was already coming around the desk. There were two other people in the waiting room and both of them were now looking. An older couple in the corner — the woman’s mouth was open. A man across from them, in a wheelchair, with his hand frozen over his crossword puzzle. None of them said anything. None of them needed to. Everyone in that waiting room — the older couple, the man with the crossword, the receptionist hurrying over with her bifocals bouncing on their chain — knew, in the way that humans simply *know* certain things, exactly what they had just seen.

Bonnie was thinking many things at once. She was thinking *my baby.* She was thinking *I just did it again.* She was thinking *I’m doing this in front of strangers.* She was thinking *those people will tell other people about this.* She was thinking *I’m going to wake up. I’m going to wake up right now.* She did not wake up. Jonah was still folded over in the chair next to her and his face was still hidden in his hands and his shoulders were shaking very small fast shakes and he was not making any sound at all.

“Jonah,” she whispered. “Jonah, baby, look at Mama.”

“*Don’t,*” he whispered back.

“Baby.”

“*Mom.* Just — just *don’t,* for a second. Just — give me a second. Please.”

She nodded. She didn’t take her hand off the back of his neck. She did not move it. She kept it there, gentle, the way she used to keep it there in the pediatrician’s office, the same hand, the same warm flat steady weight, with her thumb against the soft place behind his ear.

The receptionist reached them.

She bent down next to Bonnie. She was a woman in her sixties, with very kind eyes and an unflappable mouth, the kind of woman who had been working an urgent care intake desk for thirty years and had seen, by now, more or less everything.

“Honey,” she said. “Honey, what happened?”

“I — my purse — I turned — I swung my purse — and I — and he was sitting—”

“Okay. Okay, sweetheart. Okay.” She put her hand on Bonnie’s knee. Then she lowered her voice and bent further down. “Honey. Is this your other son?”

Bonnie nodded. Bonnie could not speak. Bonnie nodded.

The receptionist exhaled, very slowly, through her nose.

“Okay,” she said. “Okay. Okay, sweetie. Okay. Let’s — let’s get you a chair too, honey,” she said over her shoulder to Jonah. “Let’s get you back. Honey, can you walk for me? Can you stand up?”

Jonah did not answer.

“Honey?”

Jonah lifted his head finally. His face was the color of wet paper and his eyes had gone glassy and unfocused, the way Caleb’s had gone glassy and unfocused an hour and a half ago in the kitchen, and Bonnie understood, with a slow horrible clarity, that her younger son was about to vomit.

He didn’t quite make it.

It went mostly on the chair. Some went on Bonnie’s knee. The woman behind the desk did not flinch. She put her hand on his back and said, “That’s okay, honey, that’s okay, just get it out, get it all out, sweetheart.”

Bonnie stared at her son and could not remember how to breathe.

“Both of them?” the receptionist asked her, very gently.

“No,” Bonnie whispered. “No, just — just the one. Just the one. *Please* just the one.”

The receptionist patted Jonah’s back. She did not look reassured.

“Let’s — let’s get you in the back, sweetheart,” she said to him. “Let’s — let’s get a doctor on you right quick.”

## Chapter 4 — The Minivan

Both her boys were in rooms.

They were across the hall from each other. Caleb in two. Jonah in three. The doors had the little pull-down privacy curtains and both curtains were drawn. Bonnie sat in the small family chair just outside the nurses’ station, between the two closed doors, with a Styrofoam cup of bad coffee in her hand that she had not drunk from. She had not taken her eyes off the floor in twenty minutes. Her right knee was vibrating without her permission.

Dr. Vasquez came out of room two first.

She was a small dark-haired woman in her forties, with a face that looked like it had not been surprised by anything in a long time. She crouched down in front of Bonnie’s chair.

“Mrs. Mercer.”

“Yes.”

“So.” The doctor took a breath. She had the bedside-manner exhale of someone who had practiced this a lot. “I’m going to talk to you about Caleb first, all right?”

“All right.”

“Both of Caleb’s testicles ruptured. The right one is — there’s not much there. The left one — the second one — same picture. The testicles are not salvageable, either side. We’re going to need to remove them. Both. I’m so sorry. We — we’d like to get him over to County for the surgery as soon as we can. The sooner we can get the cords clamped and the sacs cleaned out, the better for him. There is a small risk of infection if we wait, and I — I want to be straight with you — your son is going to feel a lot better, sooner, on the other side of this.”

“Both of them,” Bonnie whispered.

“Both, ma’am. I’m so sorry.”

“Okay.”

“Mrs. Mercer. I want you to hear me say this. This was not your fault.”

Bonnie laughed. It was a terrible, dry, papery noise. It bore no resemblance to a laugh. The doctor did not flinch. The doctor had probably heard worse laughs in her career than that one.

“Mrs. Mercer. As for your younger son. Jonah’s right testicle is also — there is also rupture, and that side is also going to have to come out. The left side is intact. He’s going to be okay. We can — we can do his right side at the same time as Caleb’s, over at County. Same anesthesia for both boys, same OR. Get it all over with at once, get them both home recovering by tomorrow night.”

Bonnie nodded.

“I’m going to call over there now. Sit tight. I’m going to send Jonah out in a minute, he wants to see you, all right? And then we’re going to get them both transported.”

“Okay.”

“Mrs. Mercer.”

“Yes.”

“Look at me, please.”

Bonnie looked at her.

“This was not your fault.”

“Okay,” Bonnie said.

The doctor stood up. She rested her hand on Bonnie’s shoulder for a moment. Then she went back into room two and closed the door behind her, and a minute after that Jonah came out of room three in his flannel pajama pants and a paper hospital robe over his shirt, with a small ice pack wedged inside the front of his waistband and a slight stoop to him he hadn’t had this morning, and Bonnie stood up and folded him into her arms without saying anything, and he held on to her for a long time.

“They have to go,” he said, into her hair. “He has to lose them both.”

“Yes, baby.”

“And — and the one.”

“Yes, baby.”

He didn’t cry. She thought he might. He didn’t. He just rested his forehead on her shoulder and breathed, and his shoulders were narrow and warm under her hands and she could not stop feeling, with her right kneecap, the *crunch* of his brother’s left ball under her.

“They’re moving us to County in a few minutes,” he said. “Same surgery, both of us. Same time. They want me to go down to the van and wait there, the gurneys are coming in this side, they want to keep the hallway clear.”

“Okay, baby.”

“Mom.”

“Yeah, sweetheart.”

“Please. *Please* drive carefully.”

Bonnie made a noise that might have been a laugh and might have been a sob.

---

She helped him out.

Jonah was a careful walker now. He held his right elbow against his ribs and his left hand a little cupped over the front of his pajama pants and he took the parking-lot ramp one foot at a time, with his mom’s hand under his other elbow, and he didn’t talk. The sun was up properly now. It was eighty-six degrees in the lot at quarter to ten. There were patriotic flags hanging from the streetlamps. Somebody had put up a banner on the front of the urgent care that said HAPPY 4TH and had a cartoon flag on it. Bonnie wanted to tear the banner down. Bonnie wanted to find the person who had put up the banner and scream at them.

She helped Jonah down off the curb. He winced once, a small one, when his right foot hit the asphalt; the jostle reached his sack.

“Sorry,” Bonnie whispered.

“It’s okay, Mom.”

“No, baby, *I*—”

“*Mom.* It’s — it’s really okay.”

They reached the minivan. She unlocked it with the fob. The slidey side door slid open and he eyed it for a long doubtful second before deciding it would be easier to take the front passenger seat. She helped him around to the passenger side. She held the door open with her left hand and her purse — *that* purse, the tan one, with its chunky brass latch, the one she had not been able to put back over her shoulder, the one she was carrying by its strap in her hand like a dead thing — was in her right hand. She held the strap.

Jonah eased down sideways onto the passenger seat. He had to lean back at an angle to slide in, because anything that brought his knees together right now made him hiss between his teeth. He braced his hand on the dash. He got his butt onto the seat and then he had to use his hands to lift his right leg into the van one inch at a time.

“Okay,” he panted. “Okay. Okay, I’m — I’m in.”

“Let me get your belt.”

“I’ll get it, Mom.”

“No, sweetie, your hands are shaking, I’ll get it, you don’t have to — I’ve *got* it—”

She leaned across him.

This was, she would understand later, an obviously bad idea. She would understand it the way she had eventually understood the cast iron skillet. She would understand it the way she had eventually understood the runner on the stairs. She had not understood the cast iron skillet at the time, or the runner at the time, or this at the time. She leaned across her son to reach the seatbelt anchor between the seats.

She had to lean far. The anchor was on his right side, between the front seats, and he was on her left, and her arm had to go all the way across his torso and *then* down. Her bust — the full heavy weight of her, gathered in the cup of her old t-shirt — pressed against his chest as she leaned. She didn’t notice. She was looking at her hand. She was looking at the seatbelt buckle, which was in her right hand, with its metal tongue sticking down.

Her left hand braced on the seat next to him for balance.

Her left elbow, the bony point of it, the *only* bony point on her, jammed *down* into the soft warm fabric of his pajama pants between his legs as she braced.

Her elbow landed on his left ball.

The left ball — Jonah’s last ball, his solitary ball, the surviving one, the one the doctor in the urgent care had specifically said was intact — was sitting low and exposed in the front of his pajama pants because Jonah had been *carefully* arranging the ice pack against his right side, on the doctor’s instructions, and in arranging it he had inadvertently nudged the lefty out of the way to make room for the cold pack. The lefty was hanging unprotected at the front of his sack, and Bonnie’s elbow, which was the only bone she had in the front of her body, came down on it from above with most of her weight on it because her bust had outpaced her balance and she was *falling* across him.

*Squick.*

He made the inhale noise again — that small surprised *“—!”* — and then no other noise, because the seatbelt buckle in her right hand, which had been aimed at the receiver between the seats, missed the receiver entirely.

It went *past* the receiver.

The metal tongue of the buckle, sticking down out of her fist, kept going down through the gap between the seats and then, because her hand kept lowering with her weight, jabbed back *up* against the under-side of the seat, slipped off the lip, and came back forward — the way a buckle on a long retractor will spring forward when freed — and snapped, on the recoil, into the back of Jonah’s sack.

Up *into* it. From below.

The leftover momentum of her falling weight pressed her elbow down on the front of his ball at the same instant the buckle’s metal tongue clipped up against the back of it from underneath. His last testicle was, for one tiny grotesque half-second, held between her elbow and a piece of stamped steel.

*Pop.*

He went out.

Not in pain. *Out.* He went limp. Eyes rolled. Body slack against the seat. Bonnie was on top of him, half across his lap with her elbow buried in his crotch, and he sagged against the headrest with his mouth a little open and a thin trickle of drool sliding down the side of his chin.

Bonnie understood, with a kind of awful crystalline clarity, exactly what had just happened.

She pushed herself back up off her son. Slowly. Carefully. The way she would back away from something fragile she had just broken. She kept her hand against his shoulder so he wouldn’t slide forward. She heard, very distantly, herself making a noise. It was not a word. It was a noise. It rose and fell.

Jonah blinked.

He blinked once. Twice. He looked at the ceiling of the minivan. He looked at her.

“Mom,” he whispered.

“Baby.”

“Mom,” he said, and his voice cracked. “Mom, no. Mom. No no no no.”

“Honey—”

“*Both of them,*” he whispered. “*Both of them, both of them, both of them—*”

He started to cry. He cried with his eyes shut. He cried the way he had cried at six years old when she had told him their cat had died. He cried that exact same way, with his shoulders shaking and his mouth open and his hands lying limp in his lap because he couldn’t bring himself to put his hands on what was down there now, on what was *left* down there now, and Bonnie cried with him, standing in the open passenger door of her minivan in a parking lot full of Fourth of July flags, with her hand on his shoulder and her purse around her wrist and the metal seatbelt buckle still in her other hand.

A nurse came running across the lot. Dr. Vasquez wasn’t far behind her.

“What — what happened — *what happened—*”

Bonnie tried to say it. She couldn’t. She held up the seatbelt buckle, weakly, like an offering.

Dr. Vasquez took one look at Jonah and her face went very still. She turned over her shoulder and shouted, “We need another transport! *Now!*” and she pushed past Bonnie and got her hand under Jonah’s chin and looked into his eyes, and her other hand went to his wrist, and she said, “Honey, honey, look at me, look at me, I’ve got you, okay, I’ve got you, just breathe for me—”

“The other one too,” Bonnie said. She was speaking from a great distance. She watched herself say it. “The other one too. The other one, the other one, the other one.”

“I know,” the doctor said quietly. “Honey. I know.”

## Chapter 5 — After

The dual orchiectomy at County took a little under three hours.

They had to do four of them, technically, but they did the four in two patients on two adjacent tables in the same OR, and the chief urologist, who had been called in special on a holiday, was a fast careful man named Dr. Whelan who said almost nothing the whole time except *clamp, suture, suture, ligate, that’s the right one, that’s the left one, irrigate, close,* in the patient voice of a man who had done many many many of these and had no opinions about any of them. He worked on Caleb first because Caleb was older and had been hurt longer. He worked on Jonah second because Jonah was younger and was going to live with this longer than his brother and Dr. Whelan wanted to take his time.

When he came out at one-twenty in the afternoon and shook Bonnie’s hand in the family waiting area, he held it a beat longer than a doctor usually holds a mother’s hand, and he said, “Mrs. Mercer. They both did very well. They’re both stable. They’re going to be okay,” and Bonnie nodded and could not look at him because if she looked at him she would scream.

They put the boys in the same room.

It was a double on the fourth floor, with a window that looked out at the parking ramp and a TV mounted high in the corner playing a baseball game with the sound off. Caleb was in the bed by the door, Jonah in the bed by the window. They were both still groggy from the anesthesia. They were both packed in ice. They were both very pale and very young.

Bonnie sat in the chair between them.

She did not know which one to sit closer to. She finally pulled the chair into the dead center of the room between the two beds, equidistant, so that she could reach a hand out to either side, and that was how the nurse found her at three-fifteen when she came in to check Jonah’s line: a soft thick brown-haired woman in a flowery shirt with bacon grease on the cuff and a smear of dried tears down both sides of her glasses, with one hand resting on her older son’s knuckles and the other hand resting on her younger son’s knuckles, and both her sons very gently, very loosely, very automatically, holding her hands back.

Caleb woke up first. He saw her. He looked at the ceiling for a while. Then he looked at her again.

“Hi, Mama.”

“Hi, baby.”

“You crying?”

“Yes, honey.”

“Quit.”

“No, baby.”

“Mom.”

“No, baby. I’m gonna cry for a little while. I’m sorry. I can’t — I can’t help it.”

“Yeah.” He closed his eyes again. He squeezed her fingers, weakly. “Yeah, okay.”

Across the room, in the other bed, Jonah was awake too. He hadn’t opened his eyes yet. He’d just lifted his hand, slowly, and turned his palm up under hers.

“Mom.”

“Yes, baby.”

“It was an accident.”

She closed her eyes.

“It was an accident,” he said again. His voice was thick from the drugs and from the crying he had done before they put him under. “Mom. Listen. I’m only — I’m only saying this once. It was an accident. All four of them. They were all accidents. Right?”

“Yes,” she whispered.

“We love you, Mama.” He took a breath. “Don’t we, Caleb.”

“Yeah,” Caleb said, from the other bed, eyes still shut. “Yeah, Mom, we love you. It was — yeah. Yeah, it was an accident. All of them.”

“It was an accident,” Jonah said again, like he was teaching her a word. “*Accidents happen.*”

“*Stop,*” she whispered.

“*Mom.*”

“*Stop,* baby. Please.”

He stopped. He squeezed her hand. He fell asleep again, holding it.

Bonnie sat between her two boys for the rest of the afternoon, in the chair, with a hand on each of theirs, and watched the parking ramp through the window and watched the silent baseball game on the muted TV and watched her own reflection in the dark glass of the powered-down monitor, and she could not stop, for some reason, thinking of the morning. The way Caleb had yawned in the kitchen doorway. The way Jonah had said *I love you* about the coffee. The way the kitchen had smelled, faintly, of bacon fat and pancake batter, and how, for a few minutes there, before any of this, before any of it at all, she had stood at her own stove on a holiday morning with both her babies in her house at the same time for the first time in months, and she had been so, so happy that it had made her cry into the batter.

Outside, somewhere, a bottle rocket went off. A small dry distant *pop.*

Bonnie flinched.

Both her sons, in their sleep, in their separate beds, with no testicles between the two of them, squeezed her hands.

She held on.
